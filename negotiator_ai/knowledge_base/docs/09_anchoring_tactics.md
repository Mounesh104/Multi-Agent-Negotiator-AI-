# Anchoring Tactics in Negotiation

## What is Anchoring?
Anchoring is the cognitive tendency to rely heavily on the first number introduced in a negotiation. The first figure stated becomes a reference point that subsequent offers are evaluated against — even if it is unreasonable.

Research consistently shows the first anchor has an outsized effect on final outcomes, regardless of who introduces it.

## How to Anchor Effectively as a Buyer

### 1. Anchor First and Anchor Low (But Not Absurd)
Open with a number significantly below your target — typically 20–30% below your actual goal. This shifts the entire negotiation range downward.

**Rule of thumb:** Anchor at a number that is ambitious but defensible. An anchor you cannot defend with any rationale loses credibility and makes you look uninformed.

**Example:**
- Supplier quoted: ₹500/unit
- Your target: ₹440/unit (12% discount)
- Your opening anchor: ₹400/unit (20% below quote)
- Expected settlement zone: ₹420–₹445/unit

### 2. Always Anchor Before They Do
If a supplier says "let me send you our best price" — give them your number first:
> "Before you do that — we're working with a budget of [$X per unit] for this category. We'd love to see if you can work within that."

Now their "best price" is anchored against your number, not their internal price list.

### 3. Use a Precise Anchor (Not Rounded)
Precise numbers feel more researched and credible than round numbers:
- ₹437/unit feels like a calculated market rate
- ₹440/unit feels like a rounded estimate
- ₹400/unit feels like a guess

A precise anchor signals you have done your homework.

### 4. The High-Low Anchoring Sequence
Present two scenarios — one extreme, one moderate — so the moderate one seems reasonable:
> "We've seen this item as low as [$X] in the market. We don't expect that price from you — but we're hoping to land somewhere around [$Y], which we think is fair for our volume."

$Y is your actual target. $X makes $Y seem like a concession.

## Defending Against Counter-Anchors
When a supplier anchors high (or defends their original quote), counter with:

### 1. The Flinch
A visible, verbal reaction to a high anchor: "That's significantly above what we were expecting. Walk me through what drives that price?"

The flinch creates psychological pressure and invites justification — which often reveals negotiable components.

### 2. Ignore and Re-Anchor
Simply ignore their anchor and re-introduce yours:
> "I appreciate you sharing that. Our number is [$X]. Let's work from there and see if we can find common ground."

### 3. Disqualify the Anchor
Challenge the anchor's validity:
> "That quote is based on [smaller quantities / standard terms]. We're offering [larger volume / advance payment / longer commitment] — those should change the base significantly."

## Common Anchoring Mistakes
- **Anchoring so low the supplier disengages:** If your anchor is not defensible, negotiations can end before they start. Test the anchor with: "Can I explain the reasoning behind that number?" — if you can't, move it up.
- **Letting the supplier anchor first:** Always go first if you can.
- **Adjusting insufficiently from the anchor:** Research shows people anchor closer to the first number than rationality dictates — exploit this, don't fall victim to it.

## Anchoring in Practice: Full Script
> "Before we dig into your pricing structure, I want to share where we're coming from. For [X units of Y item], we're working with a target of [$Z per unit]. That's based on our market research and what we're seeing from comparable suppliers. I know that might be below your standard rate — but I'd like to use that as our starting point and see what we can do."
