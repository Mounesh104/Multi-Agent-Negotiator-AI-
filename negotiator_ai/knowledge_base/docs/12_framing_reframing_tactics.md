# Framing & Reframing in Supplier Negotiations

## What is Framing?
Framing is how you present a request, offer, or concession — the words, context, and sequence used shape how the other party evaluates it. The same economic outcome can feel like a gain or a loss depending on framing. Skilled negotiators control the frame.

## Core Framing Techniques for Buyers

### 1. Frame Your Ask as Standard Practice
Never frame a discount request as special treatment. Frame it as the normal expectation for a buyer in your situation:
> **Weak:** "Could you possibly give us a small discount?"
> **Strong:** "At this order volume, we'd expect to be in the [X–Y%] range. What does your pricing look like at that level?"

The strong version implies this is standard — the supplier must justify why you should be outside the norm, not the other way around.

### 2. Frame Price as a Partnership Investment
Reframe the conversation from "you giving us a discount" to "we're both investing in a long-term partnership":
> "We're not asking you to give something away — we're asking you to invest in a relationship that will return far more over time. If you meet us at [$X] this quarter, the volume we're projecting for Q3 and Q4 will more than make up the difference."

### 3. Frame Concessions as Reciprocal
Any time you offer a concession, name what you want in return — even if you would have given the concession anyway:
> "We're willing to move to Net 30 from Net 60 [concession from your side, even if preferred]. In exchange, we'd need the unit price at [$X]."

This establishes a pattern of reciprocity and signals you have a limited number of concessions available.

### 4. Frame the Competitor as the Benchmark, Not the Threat
Instead of threatening to switch (which can feel aggressive), frame the competitor as a market data point:
> "We're seeing [$X per unit] from other suppliers in this space. That's the market price we're benchmarking against. We'd prefer your product — can we get to that level?"

### 5. Frame Time Constraints as Opportunity (Not Urgency)
Instead of exposing your urgency: "We need this by Friday"
Frame it as opportunity: "We're in a position to finalize and pay quickly — is there a benefit you can offer for a fast close?"

## Reframing Supplier Counter-Arguments

### When Supplier Says: "Our Costs Have Gone Up"
**Reframe:** "We understand input costs fluctuate. We'd like to see the component breakdown so we can understand where the increase is and see if we can help solve it differently — substituting a component, adjusting specs, or adjusting order timing."

### When Supplier Says: "This Is Our Best Price"
**Reframe:** "We appreciate that — that tells us your list price is fixed. Let's talk about the total value of the relationship beyond list price: payment terms, delivery SLAs, bundled services. There may be ways to get to the right value for both of us."

### When Supplier Says: "We Treat All Customers the Same"
**Reframe:** "That's good to know. We're not asking for special treatment — we're asking for the price that reflects our specific situation: [volume / payment reliability / relationship tenure / competing offers]. Those factors mean we're not a 'standard' customer."

## Loss Aversion Framing
People feel losses more acutely than equivalent gains. Frame your counter-offer around what the supplier stands to lose, not just what they gain:

**Gain framing (less effective):** "If you meet our price, you'll keep our business."
**Loss framing (more effective):** "If we can't reach an agreement here, we'll need to move this volume to one of the alternatives we've evaluated — and we'd hate to lose what we've built together."

## The "Bundling" Reframe
When a supplier resists on price, reframe the negotiation to include non-price elements:
> "Let's step back from the per-unit price for a moment. If we look at the full package — price, payment terms, delivery SLA, and minimum order quantity — what would a proposal look like that works for both of us?"

This expands the negotiating space and often unlocks deals that pure price negotiations cannot.

## Framing the First Meeting vs. Renewal
- **First negotiation:** Frame as establishing a fair starting point for a relationship you intend to grow.
- **Renewal negotiation:** Frame as recalibrating terms to reflect the relationship's proven value — you've earned better terms.
