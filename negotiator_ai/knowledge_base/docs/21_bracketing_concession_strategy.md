# Bracketing & Concession Strategy

## What is Bracketing?
Bracketing is a negotiation technique where you set your opening offer so that the midpoint between your offer and the other party's offer lands at your actual target. You work backward from your target to determine your opening anchor.

## How to Calculate Your Bracket

**Formula:** Opening Offer = Your Target − (Their Offer − Your Target)

**Example:**
- Supplier quoted: ₹500/unit
- Your target: ₹440/unit
- Distance: ₹500 − ₹440 = ₹60
- Your opening offer: ₹440 − ₹60 = ₹380/unit

If negotiations converge toward the midpoint (₹440), you've hit your target. If they converge elsewhere, you still have room to move and your target remains achievable.

## The Bracketing Sequence

### Round 1: Set the Bracket
State your anchor (₹380) confidently and with justification. Do not immediately soften it.

### Round 2: The Supplier Counter
Supplier counters somewhere between ₹380 and ₹500 — say, ₹480. The bracket has now narrowed. Your next move should be a modest increase toward ₹400–410.

### Round 3: Converge Gradually
Move in decreasing increments: ₹380 → ₹405 → ₹422 → ₹432. Each move is smaller than the last. This signals you are approaching your limit.

**Why decreasing increments work:** If your moves are ₹25 → ₹17 → ₹10 → ₹8, the supplier's brain interprets the pattern as approaching your ceiling. A consistent ₹20/move signals you have unlimited room.

### Round 4: The "Final" Offer
When you're close to your target, make a slightly odd-numbered final offer (signals calculation, not room):
> "I can go to ₹437. That's genuinely the last number I can bring to my partners — they've already pushed back on this."

## The Odd-Number Strategy
Final offers with specific, non-round numbers (₹437 vs. ₹440) feel more calculated and researched:
- ₹440 feels like a round guess → supplier tests it further
- ₹437 feels like the output of a cost analysis → supplier is more likely to accept

## Linking Concessions to Variables
Never concede on price alone without connecting it to another variable:
- "I can go to ₹437, but that only works at Net 30 terms — can you confirm that?"
- "I'll move to ₹445 if you include delivery in that price."
- "At ₹437, I'd need you to commit to 48-hour lead time."

This makes each concession conditional and protects you from giving away multiple things simultaneously.

## The Strategic Concession Inventory
Before any negotiation, list every possible concession you could make — ranked by cost to you vs. value to them:

| Concession | Cost to You | Value to Them |
|---|---|---|
| Net 30 (from Net 60 preference) | Moderate | High (cash flow) |
| Monthly orders (from quarterly) | Low | High (predictability) |
| Volume increase (10% more) | Low-Moderate | High |
| Auto-pay enrollment | Very Low | Moderate |
| Written testimonial/referral | Very Low | Moderate |
| Design lock-in (packaging) | Very Low | High (production efficiency) |

Start with high-value-to-them/low-cost-to-you concessions. Save real concessions for when you need them.

## The False Concession
A false concession is something you offer as a concession that costs you nothing — and ideally something you were going to do anyway:
> "We'll commit to a consistent weekly order schedule [something you prefer anyway] if you can meet us at ₹440."

The supplier perceives value in your "commitment" even if it costs you nothing.

## Walkaway Discipline
Know your reservation price before you start. If negotiations go below it, walk away — calmly, without drama:
> "I've genuinely enjoyed this conversation, and I'd like to work with you. But at ₹480, this doesn't work for our cost structure. We'll need to go with one of the other options we've evaluated. If anything changes on your side, please do reach out."

Then actually leave — or end the call. This is the most powerful move available and the one most people can't bring themselves to use.
