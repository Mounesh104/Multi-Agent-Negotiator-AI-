# Negotiation Psychology — Cognitive Biases & How to Use Them

## Introduction
Negotiation outcomes are shaped as much by psychology as by economics. Understanding the cognitive biases that affect both buyers and sellers allows you to structure your approach to work with human psychology rather than against it.

## Key Cognitive Biases in Negotiation

### 1. Anchoring Effect
The first number stated becomes the psychological anchor for the entire negotiation. People adjust insufficiently from anchors even when they know they should.

**Use it:** State your number first. Make it ambitious but defensible.
**Defend against it:** When the supplier anchors high, explicitly acknowledge the anchor and replace it:
> "I noted your opening number. Let me share our perspective instead, because that's the range we're working from."

### 2. Loss Aversion
People feel the pain of a loss roughly 2× more intensely than the pleasure of an equivalent gain. Framing offers in terms of what the supplier *stands to lose* is more motivating than equivalent gain framing.

**Use it:**
- Gain frame (weak): "You'll get our ongoing business."
- Loss frame (strong): "If we can't reach an agreement, you'll lose a [$X/year] account to a competitor."

### 3. Reciprocity
People feel an obligation to return favors. When you make a concession, the other party feels social pressure to reciprocate — even if the concession was small or planned.

**Use it:** Make small, explicit concessions early to trigger reciprocity:
> "We're willing to move our payment terms to Net 30 [from your preferred Net 60]. We'd like to see that reflected in your price."

**Defend against it:** When a supplier makes an unsolicited concession, don't feel obligated to reciprocate immediately. Thank them and continue the negotiation.

### 4. Scarcity / Urgency Bias
People assign higher value to things perceived as scarce or time-limited.

**Use it (carefully):**
> "We're making this sourcing decision by [date]. After that, the budget is allocated and we won't be in the market again until Q[X+1]."

**Defend against it:** When suppliers create urgency ("this price is only valid until Friday"), slow down:
> "We appreciate the notice. We'll need to evaluate this at our own pace. If the price changes, we'll factor that into our comparison."

### 5. Contrast Effect
People evaluate things relative to what came before. A slightly high ask feels more reasonable if it follows an extreme ask.

**Use it:** Open with an ambitious anchor (₹380) so your real target (₹440) feels like a significant concession when you move to it.

### 6. The Endowment Effect
Once people feel they "own" something (including an agreed-to deal), they become reluctant to give it up.

**Use it:** Once you reach verbal agreement on any term, immediately confirm it:
> "Good — so we've agreed on ₹440/unit. Let's move to payment terms."

Confirming partial agreements creates psychological commitment that makes it harder to re-open.

### 7. Social Proof
People are influenced by what others do. Referencing industry norms or other buyers makes your requests feel normal, not exceptional.

**Use it:**
> "Most established buyers in our industry at this volume level are on Net 60 terms. We'd like to be treated consistently with that standard."

### 8. Commitment & Consistency
Once someone makes a commitment (even small), they feel social pressure to remain consistent with it.

**Use it:** Get small commitments early:
> "We both want this to work, right?" [Yes.]
> "And you'd prefer to keep our business rather than lose it to a competitor?" [Yes.]
> "So we're aligned that finding a workable price is in both our interests." [Yes.]

Each "yes" builds momentum toward agreement and makes it harder for the supplier to later say "no."

### 9. The Authority Heuristic
People defer to perceived authority. Citing industry benchmarks, data, or higher-level decision-makers adds weight to your position.

**Use it:** Reference your "finance team," "partners," or "industry data" even if you are the sole decision-maker:
> "Our CFO has set a target of [$X/unit] for this category based on market analysis. I'm trying to find a way to work with you within that constraint."

### 10. Framing Effects
The same option can be evaluated differently depending on whether it is framed as a gain or a loss, and whether it is compared to a high or low reference point.

**Use it:** Frame your counter-offer as a gain for the supplier:
- Weak: "Can you discount by 12%?"
- Strong: "Can we agree on [$X], which represents [volume benefit / payment reliability / long-term relationship] for you?"

## The Psychology of "Fairness"
Even when purely rational decisions would favor one party, people reject "unfair" offers — even at cost to themselves (proven by Ultimatum Game research).

**Implication for negotiations:**
- Make your requests feel reasonable and fair, not extractive.
- Explain the rationale behind every major ask.
- Acknowledge the supplier's constraints explicitly: "I understand this is a squeeze for you — which is why we're offering [something in return]."

A deal that feels fair to both parties is more durable, more likely to be honored in spirit, and more likely to lead to a good long-term relationship.
