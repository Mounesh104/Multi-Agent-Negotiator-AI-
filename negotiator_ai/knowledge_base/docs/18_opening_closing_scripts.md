# Opening & Closing a Negotiation — Scripts & Tactics

## The Opening Conversation
The opening of a negotiation sets the tone, establishes your credibility, and frames who has power. A poor opening cedes ground before you've asked for anything.

## Opening Frameworks

### Framework 1: The Prepared Buyer Open
Signal you are informed and have done your research — this alone shifts the dynamic:
> "Before we start, I want to share some context. We've done some market research on [item] pricing and we've spoken with a few other suppliers. We have a clear sense of the market range. Based on that, here's where we're starting from..."

### Framework 2: The Relationship-First Open (for renewals)
> "We've had a good relationship, and we'd like to keep it that way. As we head into renewal, we want to have an honest conversation about pricing — making sure it reflects both the relationship we've built and the market reality we're operating in."

### Framework 3: The Collaborative Open (when you need flexibility on multiple terms)
> "Rather than going back and forth on price, I'd like to have a broader conversation. Price matters, but so do payment terms, delivery reliability, and the long-term relationship. Can we look at the full picture together?"

## The Middle: Navigating Concessions

### The Concession Strategy
Plan your concessions before the negotiation:
- **Never give the first concession on price without getting something in return**
- Concessions should get smaller over time (₹50 → ₹30 → ₹15 → ₹8) — signals you're approaching your limit
- Trade non-price concessions for price concessions (e.g., "I'll move to Net 30 if you can do ₹440/unit")

### The "If You / Then I" Framework
Always link concessions explicitly:
> "If you can move to [$X], then I can commit to [Y units/month for Z months]."
> "If you can include delivery, then I can pay within [10 days]."

This signals that every concession costs you something — even if it actually doesn't.

### The Nibble (Last-Minute Add)
Just before closing, add a small request that seems minor in the context of the deal just agreed:
> "Great — and you'll include delivery in that price, right?"
> "And the payment terms are Net 45?"
> "One more thing — can you throw in [small add-on]?"

People are more agreeable when they think a deal is done. Use this for small, legitimately low-cost additions.

## The Closing

### The Trial Close
Test whether the supplier is ready to close without committing:
> "If we can agree on [$X delivered, Net 30], are we ready to proceed today?"

A "yes, but..." response tells you what the remaining obstacle is. A clean "yes" closes the deal.

### The Assumptive Close
Proceed as if agreement has been reached:
> "Let me get you our purchase order by end of day with those terms."

If they object, you learn what's unresolved. If they don't, the deal is done.

### The Either/Or Close
Give them two options, both of which work for you:
> "Would you prefer to move forward with [Option A: ₹440/unit, Net 30] or [Option B: ₹460/unit, Net 60]?"

Both options are wins for you. This avoids a yes/no decision and makes choosing feel easier.

### The Time Limit Close
Create urgency if negotiations are stalling:
> "We need to finalize our supplier for Q[X] by [specific date — 3–5 days from now]. If we can resolve this today, we're ready to place the first order immediately. If not, we'll have to go with one of our other options."

## Post-Negotiation Best Practices

### Always Confirm in Writing
After a verbal agreement: "Great — I'll send a summary email with the agreed terms. Can you confirm by end of day?"

Written confirmation prevents "I never said that" disputes and sets a clear baseline for next year's negotiation.

### End on a Positive Note
Even if you got a tough deal, thank them sincerely:
> "We really appreciate you working with us on this. We're looking forward to a strong partnership."

The supplier you beat hard today is also your emergency source when you're out of stock next month. Keep the relationship healthy.

### Set the Stage for the Next Negotiation
Close with a forward-looking signal:
> "As our volume grows over the next 6 months, we'd like to revisit this rate. Let's plan a check-in in month 5 to discuss where things stand."

## Common Closing Mistakes
- Accepting the first "final offer" (it rarely is)
- Not getting confirmation in writing
- Celebrating visibly when you get a good deal (signals you had more room)
- Burning bridges when you get a good deal (you'll negotiate again)
- Forgetting to confirm payment terms and delivery schedule alongside price
